**Behaviour Under Addiction: Dynamic Choice Between Smoking and Vaping (Job Marker Paper)**

E-cigarettes are addictive but less harmful than combustible cigarettes. Taxes on e-cigarettes may discourage vaping, but could increase more harmful cigarette demand through substitution. I study this policy trade-off using a dynamic discrete--continuous model of joint demand. Households choose which products to buy and how much to purchase. Two product-specific addiction stocks link past purchases to current choices and evolve with the quantities consumed. The model is estimated using NielsenIQ household panel data from 2019 to 2024 by maximum likelihood. A surrogate neural network approximates continuation values across states and parameter values, making estimation feasible. Addiction plays a central role in determining how households respond to price changes, differently on participation and on quantity choices. Cigarettes and e-cigarettes are substitutes: a permanent 1% increase in the e-cigarette price raises cigarette quantity by 1.4%, and a corresponding increase in the cigarette price raises e-cigarette quantity by 1.8%. Own-price responses weaken with addiction. An e-cigarette ban sends a third of vapers to smoking, whereas a cigarette ban sends almost all smokers to abstinence.

The impact of discount retail stores on household consumption and wellbeing: evidence from the UK

This paper examines whether improved access to cheaper groceries changes household purchasing behaviour, diet quality and well-being in the UK.  To account for non-random store location, I estimate an entry model relating opening decisions to local demand, neighbourhood characteristics and existing retail networks. Predicted entry probabilities inform the propensity scores used in a doubly robust difference-in-differences design. I combine dated discount-store openings in Great Britain with household purchase records and longitudinal outcomes from Understanding Society. The first discounter opening in a postcode district increases the discounter share of grocery expenditure by 5.3 percentage points, with approximately 70\% of this increase accounted for by a decline in the mainstream-chain share. The paid-price index falls by 1.04\%, accompanied by an increase in own-label purchases and a reduction in promotion spending. The decompositions indicate little change in purchased quantities and attribute most of the own-label and promotion responses to reallocation across retailers. There is no detectable change in the nutritional quality of the take-home basket. On the well-being side, average GHQ-12 and SF-12 health effects are small and statistically insignificant. Overall life satisfaction rises slightly, while financial outcomes have mixed signs. *The results suggest that improved access to discount retailing reduces the cost of grocery purchases, with no detectable dietary improvement and no consistent improvement across the survey well-being point estimates*.
